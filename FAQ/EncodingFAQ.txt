#%TITLE:Protocol Encoding FAQ

#%QUESTION:Whats involved in Encoding/Decoding SMPP PDUs?
#%ANSWER:Every SMPP PDU consists of a header and body. Each header and body consists of fields. The fields themselves can be "Integer", "C-octet string" or "Octet string" types. The basic building blocks for encoding/decoding SMPP can be designed around encode/decode methods for each of these types.

#%QUESTION:How do I Encode an Integer?
#%ANSWER:An Integer can be 1, 2 or 4 octets long and is encoded in <i>Big Endian</i> format. This means that the first octet to be encoded is the most significant octet of the integer. For example if we have a 4-octet integer with a value of 271590900. In Hex, this is 0x103025F4. Encoded as an SMPP field, it would appear as follows:<br> <pre>10 30 25 F4</pre>The format above represent the 4 octet values in hex as they are encoded.  The transmission order of the data is read from left to right. So in terms of sending this encoded integer to an SMSC, the first octet sent is 0x10, then 0x30 and so on. This is the essence of Big Endian encoding.. encode as you read it. <br> <br> The means of encoding the integer involves its separation into its 4 octets.  The easiest way of doing this is to use bit shifting and bit masking. These features are common place utilities with the C programming language. What follows are examples of how to encode integers using C.<br> <pre>unsigned int my_int = 271590900;</pre> <pre>char my_buffer[4];</pre> <pre>my_buffer[0] = (char)((my_int >> 24) & 0x000000FF);<br>my_buffer[1] = (char)((my_int >> 16) & 0x000000FF);<br>my_buffer[2] = (char)((my_int >> 8) & 0x000000FF);<br>my_buffer[3] = (char)(my_int & 0x000000FF);<br></pre> <p>To explain the above:</p> <p>The first MSB if the integer is isolated by shifting it 24 bits to the right and masking with 0xFF to zero-out any non-zero bits:</p> <pre>271590900 in Hex is 0x103025F4</pre> <p>Shifting 24 bits to the right, the MSB is moved to position of the LSB (0x??????10). The '?' indicates unknown values as bit shifting can behave differently across systems and compilers. The final stage of a bitwise AND with 0x000000FF, masks all bits except the lower 8 to 0 .. remember ? AND 0 = 0, ? AND 1 = ?. So the FF preserves the lower octet value. We now have a perfect octet (0x10) and can place it in the first slot in our char buffer.</p> <p>The encoding of the next three octets is similar but because of their positions, the bit shifting requires less movement until the final octet, which because of its position as the LSB, does not need any shifting, only bitwise masking with 0x000000FF to zero the upper octets.</p> <p>For languages that do not support bitwise shifting, think of 8 bits shift to the right as a division by 256 (each bit is a power of 2, 8 has a value of 256). So the same encoding could have been performed as follows:</p> <pre>my_buffer[0] = TRUNC(my_int / (256*256*256));</pre> <p>The function TRUNC() would be used to obtain the integer part of the result.  Some languages do not support integer division, where the remainder is discarded and as such usually provide a truncate function that returns the integer part of a real number.</p> <p>Our example of 271590900, when divided by 16777216 (256*256*256) is 16.18807912 . Discarding the remainder, we end up with 16 or in Hex 0x10. </p> <p>The encoding of the lower octets is a bit trickier. Yes we can simply divide by (256*256) and 256 to shift the appropriate octet, but we have the upper octets to still consider. </p> <p>Again referring to our example integer, when divided by 65536 (256*256), we get 4144.148254. Discarding the float and converting to Hex, we have 0x1030, so the MSB is still present. </p> <p>So assuming that the language does not have the wonderful feature of bitwise masking, we can use remainder on division or MOD.</p> <p>4144 MOD 256 = 48 or 0x30</p> <p>or</p> <p>4144 / 256 = 16.1875 .. discard 16 leaving .1875, multiply by 256 = 48 as remainder on division.</p> <p>The final octet is already in position and need only undergo the MOD to rid it of its upper three octets.</p><p>Encoding 2 and 1 octet integers involves the same steps, except that the encoding starts at 16 and 8 bit shifting instead of 24, likewise for the DIV/MOD approach.</p>

#%QUESTION:How do I Decode an Integer?
#%ANSWER:Decoding an integer is a simple reversal of the encoding as described above. In C, this would appear as follows<br> <pre>unsigned int my_int;<br>char my_buffer[] = {0x10, 0x30, 0x25, 0xF4};<br>my_int = ((my_buffer[0] << 24) & 0xFF000000) |<br> ((my_buffer[1] << 16) & 0x00FF0000) |<br> ((my_buffer[2] << 8) & 0x0000FF00) |<br> (my_buffer[3] & 0x000000FF);</pre><p>Notice this time, that we left shift to promote the octet into its correct position, the first to the MSB, the second to the next octet, needing only a 16 bit left shift and the last octet needing no shift at all as it already has the correct value. The overall value is the logical bitwise OR (or addition) of these values. The bitmasking is used to again protect the shifting from unknown bit values due to the shifting.. again this is platform and sometimes compiler dependent.</p><p> The non bitshifting languages would use multiplication by 256 for each 8 bit shift to the left. Simple addition with then do the rest.</p>

#%QUESTION:How do I Encode/Decode C-Octet Strings?
#%ANSWER:The term C-Octet string is used to describe how SMPP handles strings. They are simply sequences of ASCII characters terminated with a NULL byte (0x00). Most languages will encode strings in this manner. In C, a simple strcpy(my_pdu_buffer, my_string); will do the job, copying the string and its NULL terminator into the PDU buffer. Decoding is handled the same way, using a strcpy() call.

#%QUESTION:How do I Encode/Decode Octet Strings?
#%ANSWER:Octet strings are again sequences of octets but are not necessarily printable or NULL terminated. SMPP applications designed to send/receive binary messages carrying extended character sets, will often experience this type of encoding. The submit_sm, deliver_sm short_message fields and message_payload TLV support this type of encoding. In both cases a length field is used to dictate or measure the amount of data as a string length function is useless.. remember binary data may contain NULL bytes.. these would fool a strlen() call into believing it had met the terminator. In C, octet strings are encoded/decoded using memcpy(my_pdu_buffer, my_octet_stream, len); As for other languages, you need to consult the appropriate documentation on binary data handling.

#%QUESTION:OK, so how do I encode/decode a full PDU?
#%ANSWER:Easy.. model the encode/decode function as a series of encode/decode calls using your low-level routines to handle integers, strings etc.

#%QUESTION:How should I go about reading/writing PDUs from/to a socket connection?
#%ANSWER:With Unix, Windows or other OS's and languages, you will have a socket layer that provides a read() and write() function to allow you handle the sending/receiving of the encoded PDUs. Sending is the easy part as you simply assemble the PDU header and body and send using a write() call. Decoding is harder as you must first detect what you have received. The best recommendation for this is to consider that a header is the smallest possible PDU you can have.. 16 octets. So to read a PDU on your connection, you need to get at least 16 octets first and then decode these as an SMPP PDU Header. The first 4-octet integer is the command_length and this will tell you the overall PDU size. The second 4-octet integer indicates the SMPP operation and in effect what kind of PDU body is in use. After reading your mandatory 16 octets to constitute the PDU header, you now decrement the command_length by 16, to give you the remaining octets for the PDU body and optional parameters. Once you read this data, you can decode the remainder of the PDU according to its command_id. Ths PDU is now decoded field by field using integer, string and octet string decode routines.

#%QUESTION:How does SMPP detect corrupt PDUs.. is there a CRC check?
#%ANSWER:TCP/IP and X.25 both support Application Layer comms. At this level in the OSI model, the lower levels handle all CRC checking and packet loss detection, presenting only valid data to the higher layer applications. Basically, anything you read from a socket is 100% valid. You cannot lose random pieces of data. This will be detected at the lower layers and the appropriate NACKs sent from one peer to the other requesting a retransmission of data. Like Telnet, FTP and HTTP, SMPP does not need any form of integrity validation.. as long as a PDU is read in its entirity, it can be deemed to be valid. The only scope for error is in the SMPP PDU encoding of the SMSC or ESME.. in which case, that is a bug.
